\subsection{Dominant Alternatives}
Due to the nature of the results of \gls{upmavt} analyses, and the fact that it employs a montecarlo code, it was determined unfeasible to run proper weight sensitivity analysis (the approach from \cite{} was tested but failed miserably as the computation time is just unreasonable). For this reason this work resort to a different approach: run the montecarlo code with random weights, sampled at each run from a dirichlet distribution. This approach should be able to identify domninat alternatives, or alternatives that are widely influenced by the weight choice. The results of the random weight \gls{nsmc} runs are presented in figure \ref{fig:random_weights_dominance}.

\subsection{Critical Observations from Industry Experts}
While beyond the scope of \gls{mcda} frameworks, experts uniformly noted that technical evaluations may be shadowed by political imperatives: vendor selection frequently prioritizes geopolitical alliances and diplomatic relationships over technical or economic merits. This reality underscores the need to position such analyses as complementary decision support tools rather than definitive selection mechanisms.

Industry consultations revealed significant skepticism about SMR feasibility, first they agreed with the author suggestion that while modularity offers theoretical advantages, first-of-a-kind projects will likely encounter unanticipated challenges that may offset potential benefits. Secondly, they were concerned about vendor transparency. Experts questioned SMRs' market relevance, noting that most countries prioritize large-scale replacements for retiring capacity (e.g. replacement of coal-fired power plants in Poland). While the EPR's 1600 MWe capacity exceeds many nations' needs, the consensus identified 1000-1400 MWe as the optimal range for national deployment. SMRs were seen as potentially viable for private investors seeking smaller integrated solutions, but less suitable for utility-scale projects.

The proliferation of competing SMR designs was identified as problematic, creating decision paralysis for national entities while confusing public perception. Some observers suggested certain projects might represent "paper reactors" primarily aimed at securing funding rather than achieving commercial deployment.
