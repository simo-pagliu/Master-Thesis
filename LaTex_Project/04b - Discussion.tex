\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Weight Elicitation and Consistency Analysis}
\label{subsec:weight_consistency}

The elicitation process revealed significant variability in the quality of expert weight assessments. While ordinal consistency was ensured within each pairwise comparison group, the overall results exhibited intra-group inconsistencies that led to unsatisfactory weight spaces for some experts. Of the four experts who performed the complete elicitation, only one (E4, already familiar with MCDA) produced satisfactory and internally consistent weight spaces. The author acknowledges that these results are worth preserving, and notes that potential weaknesses in the mathematical formulation of the problem may have contributed to these issues. These concerns will be addressed in future publications. For the scope of this thesis, to demonstrate the full \gls{upmavt} methodology, the weight elicitation results are used as provided.

Expert E1 participated in the "first elicitation" that established baseline value functions for subsequent evaluations (steps 1 and 2), but demonstrated the lowest consistency in weight elicitation (step 3). The analysis therefore presents results both including and excluding E1's contributions to assess the ranking sensitivity to this outlier perspective. The sensitivity analysis demonstrates robust ranking patterns across expert configurations. E1's inclusion increases result uncertainty but does not substantially alter core rankings.

Weight space representations use horizontal bar plots, displaying allowable weight values on the x-axis and criteria on the y-axis. Expert E4's results (Figure~\ref{fig:weight_space_results}) exemplify acceptable consistency: clear weight ranges with evident criteria importance ordering and limited span. In contrast, other experts produced substantially wider ranges, with highly uncertain criterion ordering that does not reflect expected human preference patterns.

Consistency plots (Figure~\ref{fig:consistency_results}) compare declared pairwise comparison ratios (the $a$ value, computed as $1-vf(x)$) with ratios derived from optimized weights. The optimized weight ratios are presented as mean values sampled from the feasible solution space, with error bars ($\pm \sigma$) quantifying solution variability. Consistency is demonstrated when the declared value falls within the computed weight ratio range. Expert E4 shows generally good consistency across criteria. Other experts exhibit larger discrepancies, with wider error bars reflecting large weight solution spaces and poor alignment between declared preferences and computed optimal weights.

\subsection{Consensus and Uncertainty Analysis via \gls{smc}}
\label{subsec:consensus_analysis}

The \gls{smc} procedure examines expert consensus on alternative values before aggregation, revealing both consensus levels and individual expert variations. Figure~\ref{fig:smc_france_sum} presents probability histograms for each alternative under weighted sum aggregation for France (based on 2000 iterations per expert). 

Clear expert agreement is evident: all distributions occupy similar value ranges with comparable shapes. Notable individual variations exist as expected: Expert E4 values the Rolls-Royce design higher than other experts; Experts E4 and E3 assign higher values to the BWRX300. Expert E1 slightly favors the AP1000 while undervaluing the APR1400 relative to other experts. Overall consensus on alternative values remains very high, with maximum diversity appearing for the BWRX design (exhibiting two distinct probability peaks) and the Rolls-Royce design (where one expert clearly favors it above consensus).

Under geometric mean aggregation (Figure~\ref{fig:smc_france_geo}), distributions appear substantially more spread, explaining reduced dominance patterns in this method. The NuScale design exhibits a well-defined low-value peak with a long tail toward higher values; similar (though less pronounced) distributions appear for Rolls-Royce, BWRX, and APR alternatives. Conversely, AP1000 and \gls{epr} designs show wider, flatter distributions. This pattern suggests a practical interpretation: non-top alternatives exhibit well-defined (unfavorable) values readily discarded from consideration, while top alternatives present riskier profiles where the decision-maker must accept greater uncertainty. The AP1000 emerges as the lowest-risk choice, being the only top alternative without increased probability toward lower values, though its potential best-case value remains below the \gls{epr}'s possibilities.

\subsection{Compensatory Dynamics and Dominant Alternatives}
\label{subsec:compensatory_dynamics}

To examine compensatory dynamics in the decision problem and validate the selection of aggregation methodology, this study compares fully compensatory (\gls{sum}) against partially compensatory methods (\gls{geo} and \gls{har}). Given the computational infeasibility of traditional weight sensitivity analysis (prior approaches yielded unreasonable computation times), the analysis employs \gls{nsmc} with randomly sampled weights drawn from a Dirichlet distribution. This approach identifies dominant alternatives---those maintaining advantageous rankings across varying weight assignments---and evaluates whether the fully compensatory approach adequately reflects decision dynamics.

Figure~\ref{fig:aggregation_methods_comparison_france} presents ranking heatmaps for France across the three aggregation methods. Two key observations emerge: (1) dominant alternatives clearly exist, with large reactors (particularly \gls{epr} and \gls{ap}) dominating the heatmaps across all methods, and (2) significant differences arise between fully and partially compensatory approaches, especially affecting top-ranked alternatives.

The \gls{epr} design ranks first under fully compensatory aggregation but drops to second with partial compensation methods. This behavior is logical: the \gls{epr} design excels in several technical criteria (efficiency, discharge burnup) but performs poorly in others frequently weighted heavily by experts (capital cost, construction complexity). The fully compensatory \gls{sum} method allows high positive scores to fully offset low negative scores; \gls{geo} and \gls{har} penalize low scores more severely. Consequently, the author identifies partially compensatory approaches as more sound for this analysis, as they better reflect actual expert preferences and real-world trade-off constraints in nuclear reactor selection.

No clear preference distinguishes \gls{geo} from \gls{har}; both produce identical top-3 rankings, though \gls{geo} exhibits more pronounced preference differentiation with larger gaps between alternatives. Both approaches are acceptable for determining the final ranking, though importantly, this ranking reflects subtle differences: alternatives are quite close in overall value rather than clearly separated.

The general pattern consistently shows \glspl{smr} ranked below large reactors. Within the \glspl{smr} category, the Rolls-Royce design ranks highest. Among large reactors, the \gls{epr} and \gls{ap} compete for top position, with the \gls{apr} following closely.

Dominant alternative patterns may indicate bias in indicator definition, though the alternative explanation---that some designs genuinely outperform others---cannot be dismissed. Regardless, such patterns warrant consideration as potential warning signs. The author notes that several indicators inherently favor large reactors (e.g., power output), a deliberate design choice intended to provide experts freedom in preference. Contrary to initial expectations, all experts across all countries preferred large reactors to \glspl{smr}. The author anticipated that countries like Switzerland might prefer \glspl{smr}, but experts argued against this, reasoning that nuclear power is inherently a large-scale utility technology. Several experts noted that \gls{smr} technology lacks compelling arguments from a national utility perspective and may prove more attractive to private sector applications in the future.

\subsection{Overall Rankings via \gls{nsmc}}
\label{subsec:nsmc_rankings}

Figures~\ref{fig:nsmc_rankings_ch}--\ref{fig:nsmc_rankings_po} present \gls{nsmc} ranking heatmaps with geometric mean aggregation for each country under study.

A consistent pattern emerges across all countries: strong overall preference for traditional large reactor designs. This preference reflects the simple fact that these are proven technologies with extensive operational experience. The skepticism surrounding unproven \gls{smr} technology has rendered such designs the least-preferred solutions among experts.

Among large reactors, the \gls{apr} design garners the least appeal. Expert interviews clarify this finding: experts expressed substantial doubt regarding deployment viability of a Korean design in European contexts, significantly impacting licensing and supply chain indicator assessments. This illustrative demonstrates how expert judgment shaped by real-world feasibility considerations translates into technical preference structures.

\subsection{Random Weight Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{subsec:random_weights}

To further investigate alternative dominance and weight sensitivity, random weight \gls{nsmc} analysis was conducted with weights sampled from a Dirichlet distribution. This analysis directly examines how ranking stability varies across the weight space. Figure~\ref{fig:random_weights_italy} presents results for Italy across all three aggregation methods, demonstrating that large reactor alternatives maintain strong dominance even under random weight variations, confirming that these designs represent genuinely preferred solutions rather than artifacts of specific weight choices.
