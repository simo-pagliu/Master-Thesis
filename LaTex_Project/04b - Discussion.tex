\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
In this section the results presented in Section~\ref{sec:results} are discussed and interpreted. Methodological implications are also examined, highlighting strengths and limitations of the applied \gls{upmavt} framework in this context.

\subsection{Dominance Patterns and Compensatory Analysis}
\label{sec:weight_analysis}

This analysis employs \gls{nsmc} with random weight sampled from a Dirichlet distribution to achieve two complementary objectives: identifying persistent dominance patterns across weight spaces and evaluating how different aggregation methodologies affect ranking outcomes.

\subsubsection{Dominance Patterns}
A dominance pattern is observed when alternatives maintain consistent rankings across varying weight assignments. Such behaviour requires careful analytical scrutiny by the analyst. While such patterns may genuinely reflect superior performance of some alternatives over the others, they can also reveal latent indicator biases. For example, in this study, there is a clear preference towards large reactors as seen from Figures \ref{fig:random_weights_italy}--\ref{fig:nsmc_rankings_geo_all}.

This is partially a consequence of the criterion “Net Power Output” which was expected to favour \glspl{smr} in contexts like Switzerland, but instead, it systematically advantaged large reactors. Contrary to initial expectations, all specialists uniformly preferred large-scale solutions, arguing that nuclear power better serves national energy strategies as a large scale utility power source. Multiple experts suggested that \glspl{smr} will be successful in future applications within the private sector (industrial process heat or remote energy systems) rather than central grid integration, highlighting a fundamental mismatch between indicator design intentions and expert opinions on nuclear deployment.


\subsubsection{Aggregation Methodos}
Figure~\ref{fig:random_weights_italy} presents ranking heatmaps for Italy across three aggregation approaches (weighted sum, geometric mean, and harmonic mean in panels a, b, and c respectively). The results reveal persistent dominance of large reactor alternatives (particularly \gls{epr} and \gls{ap}) across all aggregation methods. A clear difference between fully compensatory (weighted sum) and partially compensatory (geometric and harmonic mean) approaches is also evident. The \gls{epr} design ranks at a solid first-place under full compensation but drops to second position with partial compensation methods. 

Table~\ref{tab:aggregation_comparison} presents the modal rankings for each reactor alternative across the three aggregation approaches in the case of Italy. The \gls{epr} and \gls{ap} exhibit sensitive ranking to the aggregation method: the \gls{epr} secures first position under fully compensatory \gls{sum} aggregation but drops to second under both partially compensatory methods, in favour of the \gls{ap}. The \gls{apr} maintains remarkable stability at third position regardless of aggregation method, while the Rolls-Royce \gls{smr} fluctuates between third and fourth places. Both smaller \glspl{smr} (\gls{bwr} and NuScale) consistently occupy the lowest ranks (5th--6th), with minor positional exchange between methods.

This behaviour is a logical consequence of its excellent technical performance (such as efficiency or discharge burnup) being offset by poor scores in criteria that receive high weights (capital cost, construction complexity). The author believes that in this case, fully compensatory methods overstate the \gls{epr} advantages and that partially compensatory methods better represent the opinions that were expressed during the expert interviews. For this reason, the final discussion will take into account partially compensatory results, in particular the \gls{geo} method which presents less dominance in the heatmaps.

\subsection{Weight Elicitation and Consistency Analysis}
\label{subsec:weight_consistency}

The elicitation process revealed significant variability in the quality of expert weight assessments. While ordinal consistency was ensured within each comparison group, the overall results exhibited intra-group inconsistencies that led to unsatisfactory weight spaces for some experts. Of the four experts who performed the complete elicitation, only one (E4, already familiar with \gls{mcda}) produced satisfactory and internally consistent weight spaces. Regardless, the author acknowledges that all results are worth preserving, and notes that potential weaknesses in the mathematical formulation of the problem may have contributed to these issues. For the scope of this thesis, to demonstrate the full \gls{upmavt} methodology, all weight elicitation results are used anyway.

Expert E1 participated in the “first elicitation” that established baseline value functions for subsequent evaluations (steps 1 and 2), but demonstrated the lowest consistency in weight elicitation (step 3). 

Weight space representations use horizontal bar plots, displaying allowable weight values on the x-axis and criteria on the y-axis. Expert E4's results (Figure~\ref{fig:weight_space_results}) exemplify acceptable consistency: clear weight ranges with evident criteria importance ordering and limited span. In contrast, other experts produced substantially wider ranges, with highly uncertain criterion ordering that does not reflect expected human preference patterns.

Consistency plots (Figure~\ref{fig:consistency_results}) compare declared pairwise comparison ratios (the $a$ value, computed as $1/vf(x)$) with ratios derived from optimized weights. The optimized weight ratios are presented as mean values sampled from the feasible solution space, with error bars ($\pm \sigma$) quantifying solution variability. Consistency is demonstrated when the declared value falls within the computed weight ratio range. Expert E4 shows generally good consistency across criteria. Other experts exhibit larger discrepancies, with wider error bars reflecting large weight solution spaces and poor alignment between preferences declared during elicitation and computed optimal weights.

\subsection{Consensus and Uncertainty Analysis via SMC}
\label{subsec:consensus_analysis}

The \gls{smc} procedure examines expert consensus on alternative values before aggregation, revealing both consensus levels and individual expert variations. Figure~\ref{fig:smc_france_sum} presents probability histograms for each alternative under weighted sum aggregation for France (based on 2000 iterations per expert).

Clear expert agreement is evident: all distributions occupy similar value ranges with comparable shapes. Notable individual variations exist, as expected: Expert E4 values the Rolls-Royce design higher than other experts; Experts E4 and E3 assign higher values to the \gls{bwrx}. Expert E1 slightly favours the \gls{ap} while undervaluing the \gls{apr} relative to other experts. Overall consensus on alternative values remains very high, with maximum diversity appearing for the BWRX design (exhibiting two distinct probability peaks) and the Rolls-Royce design (where one expert clearly favours it).

Under geometric mean aggregation (Figure~\ref{fig:smc_france_geo}), distributions appear substantially more spread, explaining reduced dominance patterns in this method. The NuScale design exhibits a well-defined low-value peak with a long tail toward higher values; similar (though less pronounced) distributions appear for Rolls-Royce, \gls{bwrx}, and \gls{apr} alternatives. Conversely, \gls{ap} and \gls{epr} designs show wider, flatter distributions. 

This pattern suggests a practical interpretation: non-top alternatives exhibit well-defined (unfavourable) values readily discarded from consideration, while top alternatives present riskier profiles where the decision-maker must accept greater uncertainty. The \gls{ap} emerges as the lowest-risk choice, being the only top alternative without increasing probability toward lower values, though its potential best-case value ($\sim 0.6$) remains below the \gls{epr}'s possibilities ($\sim 0.8$).

\subsection{Policy Implications}
\label{sec:policy_brief}
In the following paragraphs we present broader considerations derived from the analysis of the \gls{nsmc} results using geometric mean aggregation (Figures~\ref{fig:nsmc_rankings_geo_all}), which are considered most representative of expert judgments. These insights aim to inform policymakers on strategic directions for nuclear energy deployment in Western Europe.

\subsubsection{Most Important Criteria}
“Capital investment costs” and “supplier availability” emerged as universally critical criteria among all but one expert, while “load-following capabilities” were uniformly dismissed as irrelevant for nuclear applications. Social acceptance factors revealed significant polarization: some experts considered \gls{ghg} reduction and nuclear waste produced insufficient to sway public opinion, while others viewed these as fundamental to gaining societal approval. Economic benefits to electricity markets represented the sole universally acknowledged public concern.

Technical parameters, particularly thermal efficiency and discharge burnup, were consistently deemed irrelevant from a policy perspective. Conversely, construction complexity and design complexity (serving as proxies for potential construction delays and manufacturing challenges respectively) received high importance ratings, suggesting these qualitative indicators warrant more rigorous quantification in future assessments.

\subsubsection{Technology Preferences and Regional Patterns}

Contrary to initial hypotheses, experts across all countries demonstrated strong, uniform preference for established large reactor designs, reflecting their proven operational track records. This consensus extends even to Switzerland, where smaller designs were expected to gain traction. Small modular reactors consistently ranked lowest, with experts citing lack of demonstration as the primary limitation for current deployment considerations.

Among large reactor options, the \gls{apr} design received the least favour due to perceived deployment challenges in European contexts. Expert interviews revealed multifaceted concerns including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Political barriers to Korean-designed reactors
    \item Licensing difficulties arising from divergent safety standards
    \item Supply chain integration challenges
\end{itemize}

This pattern underscores how real-world feasibility considerations fundamentally shape technical preferences in expert judgments.

Subtle national variations emerge in the lower rankings: Italy and Poland both favor the Rolls-Royce \gls{smr}, while France and Switzerland demonstrate preference for the \gls{bwrx} design. Particularly notable is the case of Switzerland where the \gls{bwrx} shares third position with the \gls{apr} design. These country-specific differences remain confined to secondary alternatives and exhibit limited magnitude, suggesting consistent preferences across Western European contexts.

\subsubsection{System-Level Considerations and Strategic Recommendations}

While it is clear that reactors dominate national energy strategies across Western Europe, this convergence presents long-term grid stability challenges. The 2025 Spanish blackout exemplifies risks associated with inflexible base load capacity in systems with growing renewable penetration. As nuclear plants face increasing pressure to reduce capacity factors, both economic viability and operational lifetimes may be compromised.

Policymakers should adopt a pan-European perspective when planning nuclear capacity expansions, prioritizing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Grid flexibility and resilience metrics that take into account cross-border interdependencies
    \item Balanced deployment strategies that account for interconnection capabilities
    \item Stability assessments that transcend national boundaries
\end{itemize}

The current trajectory toward large-reactor fleets may undermine continental energy security if flexibility requirements are overlooked in the transition to high-renewable systems.
