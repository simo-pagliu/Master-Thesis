\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Key observations regarding the elicitation process, expert behavior, and methodological performance are discussed to provide insights into the strengths and limitations of the approach.

\subsection{Elicitation}
The initial "first elicitation" required 3.5 hours, while subsequent sessions averaged under 2.5 hours, validating the efficiency gains predicted in Sections~\ref{sec:value_functions} and~\ref{sec:qualitative_indicators_uncertainty} when using a baseline elicitation as reference.

A methodological compromise was necessary for the \gls{pilebwt} process. Ideally, comparisons should have been country-specific to account for varying indicator ranges (country-dependent indicators). To balance rigor with practical constraints, all comparisons used unified ranges spanning minimum-maximum values across all countries. Post-elicitation, country-specific values were derived through linear mapping to preserve relative relationships. This approach maintains value function validity within each national context, unlike range clipping that could distort relationships. While no significant bias is expected from this simplification, dedicated validation studies would be valuable to confirm this assumption.

\subsection{Inconsistency Management}
Weight elicitation revealed substantial variability in expert consistency. E3 demonstrated exceptional judgment coherence, likely reflecting their experience with \gls{mcda} methods. Other experts showed moderate inconsistency, with E1 exhibiting the most pronounced deviations. As anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:results}, while E1's inclusion noticeably increased result uncertainty, it produced no fundamental shifts in the final rankings. While this could suggest robustness in the \gls{upmavt} method against inconsistent inputs, it is more likely that the set of alternatives present a clear hierarchy that disregards weight fluctuations.

The optimization-based \gls{pilebwt} method proved sensitive to even minor judgment inconsistencies. This analysis enforced cardinal consistency, requiring consistent indicator ordering between best and worst comparisons. For experts E2 and E4, marginal inconsistencies (e.g., declared 0.42 when $\geq$ 0.45 was required) were resolved through minimal judgment adjustments (to 0.45 in this case). Expert E1 presented more substantial inconsistencies that resisted simple correction, resulting in expanded, and clearly unreasonable, weight spaces.

As anticipated in \ref{sec:results}, to assess robustness, results are presented both with and without E1's contributions, explicitly demonstrating how such inconsistencies affect final rankings while maintaining methodological transparency.

It is important to note that the present \gls{nsmc} results assign equal influence to all experts. However, consistency metrics suggest more reliable judgments (e.g., E3's contributions) might warrant greater weight.

\subsection{Observations on Expert Perspectives}
\label{sec:expert_observations}
Elicitation sessions revealed fundamental alignment in expert judgment frameworks, with divergences emerging primarily from differences in thier specialized knowledge. This pattern was particularly pronounced in \gls{qi} assessments, where experts enjoyed greater interpretive freedom. Contextual awareness proved decisive: technological specialists often rated licensing statuses more favorably than colleagues attuned to country-specific political realities.

The \gls{smr} evaluations demonstrated how "insider" knowledge significantly influences outcomes. While most experts assigned similar baseline ratings, those with direct technology experience consistently applied more conservative assessments, reflecting awareness of recent, not well known, operational challenges. This underscores the value of multidisciplinary expert panels that incorporate both technical and contextual perspectives.

Expert feedback highlighted two \glspl{qi} requiring particular attention. The "design complexity" metric faced criticism for its qualitative nature, with specialists suggesting it could be quantified through objective parameters like safety system counts, modularity degrees, and redundancy levels.

The "construction complexity" indicator, initially presented as country-dependent, was judged by most to be design-driven rather than location-specific. Experts concurred with the study's neutral baseline position on construction approaches, noting that while modularity offers theoretical advantages, first-of-a-kind projects will likely encounter unanticipated challenges that may offset potential benefits. This consensus reflects skepticism about modular construction in real-world deployment.

While beyond the scope of \gls{mcda} frameworks, experts uniformly noted that technical evaluations may be shadowed by political imperatives: vendor selection frequently prioritizes geopolitical alliances and diplomatic relationships over technical or economic merits. This reality underscores the need to position such analyses as complementary decision support tools rather than definitive selection mechanisms.

Industry consultations revealed significant skepticism about SMR feasibility, primarily concerning vendor transparency. Experts questioned SMRs' market relevance, noting that most countries prioritize large-scale replacements for retiring capacity (e.g. replacement of coal-fired power plants in Poland). While the EPR's 1600 MWe capacity exceeds many nations' needs, the consensus identified 1000-1400 MWe as the optimal range for national deployment. SMRs were seen as potentially viable for private investors seeking smaller integrated solutions, but less suitable for utility-scale projects.

The proliferation of competing SMR designs was identified as problematic, creating decision paralysis for national entities while confusing public perception. Some observers suggested certain projects might represent "paper reactors" primarily aimed at securing funding rather than achieving commercial deployment.
