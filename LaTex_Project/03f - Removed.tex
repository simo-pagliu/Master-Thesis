\subsection{Notable examples of Discarded Indicators}
\label{sec:indicators_removed}
In this section we reason about some discarded indicators that among all, require further explanation. Any other that is not present in this section was discarded for trival reasons.

While CDF values demonstrate marginal differences between designs, all modern reactors exhibit exceptionally high safety performance in absolute terms. Comparative energy safety data underscores this point: nuclear energy's fatality rate of 0.03 deaths per TWh places it among the safest energy sources, comparable to wind (0.04) and solar (0.02), and 2-3 orders of magnitude safer than fossil fuels (2.82 for gas, 32.72 for brown coal) \cite{Markandya_Wilkinson_2007, SOVACOOL20163952, OurWorldInData_EnergyDeathRates_2025}. Radiation exposure data from the US EPA \cite{EPA_Radiation_Sources_2025}, based on NCRP measurements \cite{NCRP_Report_160_2009}, reveals that 48\% of average annual radiation dose comes from medical procedures, 37\% originates from natural background radiation and less than 0.2\% is attributable to occupational/industrial sources, including nuclear power plants. Moreover, techincal safety metrics are considered to have limited influence on public opinion when risk perception prevails over risk assessment.

\subsubsection{Public surveys}
During this study's execution, multiple public surveys were reviewed based on the initial assumption that such instruments could provide quantitative measurements of qualitative factors such as public support for nuclear installations. However, the analysis exposed frequent methodological limitations introducing bias into the results. In Italy, a 2024 survey reported 81\% opposition to nuclear power \cite{IPSOS_Nuclear_Survey_2024}. However, analysis of the survey report reveals problematic question design. The primary question was phrased as: \textit{"The debate on nuclear energy has returned to the forefront. What is your opinion on the matter?"} with response options structured as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A1: "The current situation is complex; a return to nuclear should be evaluated"
    \item A2: "Nuclear has more risks than benefits and is not the right path"
    \item A3: "Nuclear energy entails excessive hidden costs and is not advantageous"
    \item A4: "Only safer technologies than current ones should be considered"
\end{itemize}

This framing demonstrates clear bias: two options (A2 and A3) explicitly oppose nuclear, A1 offers only conditional support, and A4 introduces safety concerns before suggesting potential support. Notably, the 81\% opposition figure includes A4 responses, which represent conditional rather than absolute opposition. When considering only explicit opposition (A2+A3), the figure decreases to 43\%.\\

Another study from 2024 presents a more methodologically sound approach, though its presentation emphasized positive findings. The actual data reveals a more nuanced picture: over 80\% of respondents consider themselves insufficiently informed about nuclear topics, and further investigation found that approximately 90\% were unaware of \glspl{smr} or Generation IV reactors \cite{SWG_Nuclear_Survey_2024}. These findings suggest that public opinion on nuclear energy may be more influenced by lack of information than by firmly held positions.

\paragraph{Water usage indicators}
Use of water is certantly a critical aspect to be considered when deploying a \gls{npp}, as noted mutilple times by several experts when asked about feedback on the indicator list presented in this work. However, multiple reasons determined their exclusion. The main one is that the parameters would be intrinsically site dependant since the overall water usage will be determined by the cooling solution implemented and its boundary conditions. \glspl{npp} require cooling for the condensation part of the rankye cycle, for this some plants use cooling towers, other rely on rivers to dissipate the heat or the sea and sometimes these solutions are combined. Water related indicators are therefore out of scope for this study, but they are raccomented for future implementations that might want to consider site specific conditions. 

\paragraph{Land usage indicators}
Similar to water usage, land usage is a parameter that is highly site specific. While \glspl{npp} generally have a small land footprint compared to renewable energy sources, the actual land usage can vary significantly based on site conditions, safety buffer zones, and associated infrastructure. Therefore, land usage indicators were excluded from this analysis but are recommended for future studies that incorporate site-specific evaluations.

\paragraph{Indicators related to construction time}
Estimated construction time was collected for all reactors, and found to be all between 3 to 5 years (\gls{ge} reports a construction time between 2 and 3 years for the \gls{bwrx} \cite{GEVernova_Nuclear_2025}), but from analysis of previous projects is it clear that assuming these construction times would not be realistic \cite{nea2020unlocking}. As better approach, the use of a "risk of delays" indicators was suggested by an expert in the field of risk assesment. To do so, in this study, a significantly simplified and qualitative approach was used to determine complexity of manufacturing and construction (Section \ref{sec:economic_indicators}).

\paragraph{More social aspects}
While some researchers have proposed incorporating additional social dimensions such as landscape impact or job creation potential, these, and similar factors, were again excluded since they are mostly site dependent and cannot be generalized. The potential influence of \gls{nsss} suppliers on social acceptance was acknowledged as a valid concern. For this study the exclusion of Russian and Chinese suppliers was considered an essential requirement for the final selection rather then a possible point of discussion. For this reason it was implemented \textit{a priori} during the alternative selection process (Section \ref{sec:selection_reactors}). Additionally, recent developments (2025-2026) were deliberately omitted as may not reliably represent long-term trends. Incorporating such factors would require in-depth analysis and geopolitical expertise beyond the scope of this study.

\paragraph{System managment}
Another interesting argument suggested by several researchers, is the consideration of wider system response to the deployment of nuclear reactors, such as the grid capabilities, these were excluded beacuse once again they are mostly dependant on site specific conditions. Other aspects like supply chain and waste managment problems scale up with the number of reactors deployed rather then the specific technology chosen.

\paragraph{Proliferation Concerns}
Proliferation risk is a reasonable concern when dealing with new nucelar installations, for this thesis the setting in european countries make the risk of proliferation less relevant then it could have been in other geopolitical contexts. Moreover the technologies considered in this study all \glspl{lwr} using \gls{leu} fuel, therefore even if the depolyment of several small \glspl{npp} could in principle increase the risk of proliferation, the setting and selections for the present case study make such concern irrelevant.
