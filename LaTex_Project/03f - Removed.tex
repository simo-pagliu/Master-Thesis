While CDF values demonstrate marginal differences between designs, all modern reactors exhibit exceptionally high safety performance in absolute terms. Comparative energy safety data underscores this point: nuclear energy's fatality rate of 0.03 deaths per TWh places it among the safest energy sources, comparable to wind (0.04) and solar (0.02), and 2-3 orders of magnitude safer than fossil fuels (2.82 for gas, 32.72 for brown coal) \cite{Markandya_Wilkinson_2007, SOVACOOL20163952, OurWorldInData_EnergyDeathRates_2025}. Radiation exposure data from the US EPA \cite{EPA_Radiation_Sources_2025}, based on NCRP measurements \cite{NCRP_Report_160_2009}, reveals that 48\% of average annual radiation dose comes from medical procedures, 37\% originates from natural background radiation and less than 0.2\% is attributable to occupational/industrial sources, including nuclear power plants.

Therefore, considering the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item All evaluated reactors meet comparable, high safety standards
    \item Nuclear energy demonstrates superior safety performance relative to conventional energy sources
    \item Technical safety metrics have limited influence on public perception and reactor preference, where risk perception often diverges from actual risk assessment
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Public surveys}
During this study's execution, multiple public surveys were reviewed based on the initial assumption that such instruments could provide quantitative measurements of qualitative factors such as public support for nuclear installations. However, the analysis exposed frequent methodological limitations introducing bias into the results. In Italy, a 2024 survey reported 81\% opposition to nuclear power \cite{IPSOS_Nuclear_Survey_2024}. However, analysis of the survey report reveals problematic question design. The primary question was phrased as: \textit{"The debate on nuclear energy has returned to the forefront. What is your opinion on the matter?"} with response options structured as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A1: "The current situation is complex; a return to nuclear should be evaluated"
    \item A2: "Nuclear has more risks than benefits and is not the right path"
    \item A3: "Nuclear energy entails excessive hidden costs and is not advantageous"
    \item A4: "Only safer technologies than current ones should be considered"
\end{itemize}

This framing demonstrates clear bias: two options (A2 and A3) explicitly oppose nuclear, A1 offers only conditional support, and A4 introduces safety concerns before suggesting potential support. Notably, the 81\% opposition figure includes A4 responses, which represent conditional rather than absolute opposition. When considering only explicit opposition (A2+A3), the figure decreases to 43\%.\\

Another study from 2024 presents a more methodologically sound approach, though its conference presentation emphasized positive findings. The actual data reveals a more nuanced picture: over 80\% of respondents consider themselves insufficiently informed about nuclear topics, and further investigation found that approximately 90\% were unaware of \glspl{smr} or Generation IV reactors \cite{SWG_Nuclear_Survey_2024}. These findings suggest that public opinion on nuclear energy may be more influenced by lack of information than by firmly held positions.

\subsubsection{Water usage indicators}
Use of water is certantly a critical aspect to be considered when deploying a \gls{npp}, as noted mutilple times by several experts when asked about feedback on the indicator list presented in this work. However, multiple reasons brough us to not consider water usage. The main reason is that this parameter is extremly site dependant since the overall water usage will be heavily dependent on the cooling solution implemented and its boundary conditions. \glspl{npp} require cooling for the condensation part of the rankie cycle, for this some plants use cooling towers, other rely on rivers to dissipate the heat or the sea and sometimes these solutions are combined. Water related indicators are therefore cosnidered out of scope for this study, but they are raccomented for future implementations that might want to consider site specific conditions. 

\subsubsection{Indicators related to construction time}
Estimated construction time was collected for all reactors, and found to be all between 3 to 5 years (\gls{ge} reports a construction time between 2 and 3 years for the \gls{bwrx} \cite{GEVernova_Nuclear_2025}), but from analysis of previous projects is it clear that assuming these construction times woudl not be realistic \cite{nea2020unlocking}. The best approach was deemed to consider risk of delays rather then estiamted building time. To do so, in this study, a significantly simplified approach was used, suggestions on better approches are raccomented for future works in Section \ref{sec:suggestions}.

\subsubsection{More social aspects}
While some researchers have proposed incorporating additional social dimensions such as landscape impact or job creation potential, these, and similar factors, were excluded since they are mostly site dependent and cannot be generalized. The potential influence of \gls{nsss} suppliers on social acceptance was acknowledged as a valid concern. For this study the exclusion of Russian and Chinese suppliers was considered an essential requirement for the final selection rather then a possible point of discussion. For this reason it was implemented \textit{a priori} during the alternative selection process (Section \ref{sec:selection_reactors}). Additionally, recent developments (2025-2026) were deliberately omitted as may not reliably represent long-term trends. Incorporating such factors would require in-depth analysis and geopolitical expertise beyond the scope of this study.

\subsubsection{System managment}
Another interesting argument that researchers suggested is the consideration of wider system response to the deployment of nuclear reactors, such as the grid capabilities, these were excluded beacuse once again they are mostly dependant on site specific conditions. Others like supply chain and waste managment problems scale up with the number of reactors deployed rather then the specific technology chosen.

\subsubsection{Proliferation Concerns}
Proliferation risk is a reasonable concern when dealing with new nucelar installations, for this thesis the setting in european countries make the risk of proliferation less relevant then it could have been in other geopolitical contexts. Moreover the technologies considered in this study all \glspl{lwr} using \gls{leu} fuel, therefore even if the depolyment of several small \glspl{npp} could in principle increase the risk of proliferation, the setting and selections for the present case study make the concert irrelevant.
