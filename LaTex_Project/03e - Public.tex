\subsection{Sociopolitical Indicators}
The last category of indicators regards all aspects that are considered to be of most importance to the general public. The main rationale behind the choice of these indicators is to capture aspect that can most likely end up in public debates and therefore influence the public opinion.

\paragraph{Potential electricity market benefit}
\label{sec:red_elect_price}
To assess the potential consumer impact of nuclear reactor deployment, this analysis evaluates its effect on electricity pricing. The investigation proceeds from the assumption that all examined markets operate under marginal pricing mechanisms, which accurately describes France, Poland, and Italy, and largely applies to Switzerland as well, despite some additional price adjustment measures. In marginal pricing markets, the wholesale price of electricity is determined through a bidding process, where the final price equals the cost of the last generating source required to meet demand. For instance, if a bidding zone requires 100 MW of capacity with renewables only able to supplying 80 MW and gas generation covering the remaining 20 MW, the electricity price of the zone will reflect the cost of gas-powered generation.


The first step involves identifying the marginal generating source and quantifying its contribution to total electricity production. This assessment utilized country-specific data from the \gls{iea} Electricity Overview \cite{IEA_Electricity_2024} and the \gls{entso} Transparency Platform \cite{ENTSOE_Transparency_2025}, which also provided annual electricity cost data. All referenced data pertain to 2024, and the complete analytical methodology is available in the accompanying repository (see Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_code}).

Annual energy production for each reactor was then calculated by applying a capacity factor distribution to the nameplate capacity. The analysis employs Equation \ref{eq:energy_yearly} with capacity factors assumed with a distribution representative of the average performance of existing nuclear reactors in Europe (see Section \ref{sec:uncertainty_analysis}).


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:energy_yearly}
E_{reactor,yr} [GWh] = P_{e}[MW_e] \cdot 8760 [h] \cdot CF [-] \cdot \frac{1}{1000}
\end{equation}

The price impact evaluation uses France as the reference case, representing Europe's only nuclear-saturated electricity market. Consequently, all reactor designs yield a zero price reduction for France, as expected in a market where nuclear already dominates the generation mix.

For other countries, the analysis calculates potential price reductions by comparing each nation's 2024 average electricity cost against France's lower nuclear-based pricing. The projected benefit is then adjusted by the number of reactor units required to fully displace the current marginal generating source. The final results express the potential annual reduction in average electricity prices in $EUR/MWh$.

\paragraph{Potential Reduction of Greenhouse Gas Emissions}
\label{sec:ghg_red}
Potential greenhouse gas emission reductions were quantified by comparing the average emissions from each country in 2024 with projected reductions following deployment of one reactor unit. Country-specific emission intensities were derived from Electricity Maps aggregated data \cite{ElectricityMaps_CarbonIntensity_2024}, while total load figures were obtained from the \gls{entso} Transparency Platform \cite{ENTSOE_Transparency_2025}.

For nuclear generation's lifecycle emissions, the analysis adopts Liu et al.'s (2023) global meta-analysis of 42 studies \cite{10.3389/fenrg.2023.1147016}, which reports a worldwide average of 15.815 $\pm$ 3.59 gCO$_2$-eq/kWh for light water reactors. This conservative estimate was selected despite context-specific variations: French nuclear operations typically demonstrate lower intensities (2 $\sim$ 6 gCO$_2$-eq/kWh) according to both operational data \cite{paillere_2021} and IAEA assessments \cite{IAEA_Climate_Change_Nuclear_Power_2022}, with some French reactor studies reporting values up to 12 gCO$_2$-eq/kWh \cite{paillere_2021}. The global average was preferred as a conservative estimate.

\glspl{smr} were assigned a slightly higher emission intensity of 17.24 $\pm$ 3.91 gCO$_2$-eq/kWh, reflecting Carless et al.'s (2016) finding that \glspl{smr} demonstrate approximately 9\% higher lifecycle emissions than conventional large \glspl{lwr} \cite{CARLESS201684}.

\paragraph{Spent Nuclear Fuel Production}
Given public sensitivity regarding nuclear waste, this analysis includes \gls{snf} production as a key indicator, quantified in tons and normalized per 1 GW-annum to facilitate comparison of waste generation efficiency across reactor designs. Spent fuel estimates were calculated through material balance as outlined in equations \ref{eq:snf_production_step1} through \ref{eq:snf_production_step4}, deriving the values from each reactor's thermal efficiency and discharge burnup parameters. The variables employed are presented in Table \ref{tab:snf_variables}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lXl}
        \textbf{Variable} & \textbf{Description} & \textbf{Unit} \\\hline\\[-9px]
        $Bu_d$ & Discharge burnup & $\left[\frac{GWd}{tonHM}\right]$ \\[2px]
        $\eta$ & Thermal efficiency & $[\%]$ \\[2px]
        $Bu_{d,e}$ & Discharge burnup in electrical terms & $\left[\frac{GWd_e}{tonHM}\right]$ \\[2px]
        $ Bu_{d,e}^* $ & Annual discharge burnup & $\left[\frac{GWa_e}{tonHM}\right]$ \\[2px]
        $ SNF_{prod}^* $ & Mass of metal in spent nuclear fuel discharged per GWa produced & $\left[\frac{tonHM}{GWa_e}\right]$ \\[2px]
        $ SNF_{prod} $ & Mass of spent nuclear fuel (oxide) produced per GWa produced & $\left[\frac{tonUO_2}{GWa_e}\right]$ \\
        $ MM_{UO_2} $ & Molar mass of uranium dioxide & $\left[\frac{g}{mol}\right]$ \\[2px]
        $ MM_U $ & Molar mass of uranium & $\left[\frac{g}{mol}\right]$ \\[2px]
    \end{tabularx}
    \caption{Variables used in the computation of Spent Nuclear Fuel production}
    \label{tab:snf_variables}
\end{table}

\textbf{Step 1}: Convert discharge burnup to electrical terms
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:snf_production_step1}
\quad Bu_{d,e} = Bu_d \cdot \eta
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 2}: Convert from daily to annual units
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:snf_production_step2}
\quad Bu_{d,e}^* = \frac{Bu_{d,e}}{365}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 3}: Calculate the mass of heavy metal discharged per GWa produced
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:snf_production_step3}
\quad SNF_{prod}^* = \frac{1}{Bu_{d,e}^*}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 4}: Convert from heavy metal mass to oxide mass
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:snf_production_step4}
\quad SNF_{prod} = SNF_{prod}^* \cdot \frac{MM_{UO_2}}{MM_U}
\end{equation}


\paragraph{Perceived Safety}
Finally, the perceived safety from the general public point of view was considered. The reason behind the exclusion of engineering safety parameters is detailed in section \ref{sec:indicators_removed}. In short, for this analysis we recognize that, from an engineering standpoint, all reactors can be considered safe. Nonetheless, from the history of nuclear power it is evident that the opinion of the public is much more important than the one of probabilistic risk assessments (see Section \ref{sec:history_nuclear_power} and \ref{sec:country_selection}). This study therefore tries to quantify the opinion of the public on different reactor designs, mostly based on their potential appearance in the media. The first assumption that the author was confident to make is that any large "traditional" reactor will be judged the same way, so the difference might lie between the broad category of "large" reactors and \glspl{smr} models. To have a baseline understanding, a small survey was conducted presenting the three \glspl{smr} models by the key points that their own suppliers use, Rolls-Royce is mostly focused on the modularity, size and flexibility while Nuscale and \gls{ge} emphasize, in slightly different ways,  the safety advantages of their integrated design, mostly the ability to shut down safely without human intervention (which is something that all commercial reactors do anyway) and the ability to cool down without external power. For each design, the participants were asked to say if they believed the reactor was safer, as safe as, or less safe than traditional large reactors. The survey also provided images of the reactor to better represent the way they might appear in the media. The results of this small survey (n=50) show that both Nuscale and the \gls{bwrx} were perceived as safer than traditional large reactors by a majority while Rolls-Royce \gls{smr} was perceived as safer by a minority of participants (only one respondent thought it was less safe, for this analysis this response was grouped with the "as safe as" category). A detailed presentation of the survey can be found in appendix \ref{sec:appendix_survey}, the results are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Nuscale: 62\% safer, 38\% as safe as
    \item \gls{bwrx}: 62\% safer, 38\% as safe as
    \item Rolls-Royce \gls{smr}: 30\% safer, 70\% as safe as
\end{itemize}
If we state that the amount of people that think that nuclear reactors are safe is $x$ we can create the distribution presented in Table \ref{tab:safety_distribution} for each reactor type.

While it is obvious that the lowest score ("unsafe") will correspond to a value of 0 and to the highest ("safer") the value function shall return 1, we cannot assume linearity for the middle ground, this information will be elicited from experts through value functions elicitation (see Section \ref{sec:value_functions}).

The value of $x$ is unknown, and will be treated as an uncertain parameter sampled from a uniform distribution between 0.3 and 0.7 to capture both a generally pro-nuclear and a generally anti-nuclear public opinion. Public surveys were not employed in this regard for reasons disclosed in section \ref{sec:indicators_removed}.
